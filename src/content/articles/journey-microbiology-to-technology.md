I have a degree in Microbiology.

Not Computer Science. Not Software Engineering. Microbiology — the study of the smallest living things.

And yet here I am, building technology businesses, writing serverless APIs, and deploying static sites to a global edge network.

This is the story of how that happened, and why I don't think it was an accident.

## The Starting Point

At the University of Benin, I studied Microbiology. The work fascinated me — the invisible systems that determine everything from disease to fermentation to ecosystems. But I found myself increasingly drawn to a different angle: **how do we understand these systems?**

The answer, of course, was data. Massive amounts of it. Genomes. Expression profiles. Protein interactions. Thousands of numbers describing a single cell's state.

And the more I looked at data, the more I realized I wasn't really a microbiologist. I was a **systems person** who happened to be looking at biology.

## Learning to Compute

I taught myself Python. Then SQL. Then enough statistics to be dangerous. I started building small pipelines — scripts that took raw data from one source, cleaned it, and produced something interpretable.

That's when it clicked. **Biology is a data problem.** The questions are biological; the answers are computational.

I built a cancer biomarker discovery platform. Then a microbiome analysis pipeline. Then an epidemiological surveillance tool. Each one taught me the same lesson in a different way: **the ability to structure, transform, and reason about data is the same skill whether the data describes a cell or a customer.**

## From Research to Systems

Somewhere around my third research pipeline, I realized I was no longer a biologist who coded. I was an engineer working in biology.

That shift changed everything.

I started asking engineering questions:

- How does this system scale?
- What happens when the data is 100× larger?
- How do I make this reproducible?
- How do I make this fast?
- How do I make this deployable?

And I realized those questions had the same answers regardless of domain — **patterns that repeat across every kind of system.**

## The Leap to Business

PrimeShieldHub began as a business need — commerce for premium gadgets in Nigeria. But the moment I tried to solve it with software, I found the same problems I'd seen in research:

- Data integration across sources
- Reliable state management
- Payment reconciliation (the business equivalent of transaction logging)
- Trust and auditability
- Real-time visibility

The same skills. Different domain.

I built PrimeShieldHub on Cloudflare Workers. Then BuildMart, its enterprise sibling. Then Savflux, this lab.

## What I Actually Learned

Microbiology didn't teach me to write code. It taught me something more useful: **how to think in systems.**

A cell is a system. A business is a system. A serverless API is a system. A design system is a system. Each has:

- Inputs and outputs
- Feedback loops
- Failure modes
- Emergent behavior
- Boundaries and interfaces

Once you've learned to see systems — really see them — the domain almost doesn't matter. You can pick up the specifics of any field in months. What you can't pick up in months is the ability to reason about structure.

## The Next Chapter

Savflux is where this trajectory becomes explicit. Software. AI. Data. Cloud. Digital systems. Research.

It's not a departure from microbiology — it's a continuation of the same instinct. The instinct to look at a complex thing and ask: **how does this actually work, and how could it work better?**

That instinct works in every domain. That's the point.
